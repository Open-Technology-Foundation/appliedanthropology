# Samin Exiled, Samin Grows Stronger

**By Petrik Matanasi**
*Historia.ID*
*3 minute read*

## Presence at the Blora Festival

Their presence at the Blora Seabad Pram Festival held at the Blora Regent's Hall on February 7 attracted the attention of other visitors. Not only their innocent behavior, but also their humble appearance. Such are the followers of Samin teachings. Although once branded as rebels, the Samin people are now beginning to be considered great figures.

## Origins of the Movement

Samin teachings (Saminism) was originally a passive resistance movement of the people in Blora led by Raden Kohar alias Samin Surosentiko against the Dutch East Indies colonial government's efforts to commercialize forest products. Because they lived as farmers and depended on nature, government policies were considered destructive to natural forests which in turn would disrupt their lives.

"Initially, this Samin movement was not messianic in nature and did not carry out aggressive actions against the government. However, entering the 20th century, its actions tended to increase and become messianic. Its advocates were mainly dominated by landowner elements who then succeeded in attracting many followers from relatively poorer farmers in line with the widespread implementation of the taxation system," wrote Marwati Djoened Pusponegoro et al. in *National History of Indonesia: The Emergence of Colonization in Indonesia, 1700-1900*.

## Passive Resistance and the Just King

Their resistance tended to be passive because it was carried out without physical violence like the followers of Mahatma Gandhi in India, namely by refusing to pay taxes. The increasing number of followers made Samin Surosentiko, who had been spreading his teachings in Klopodhuwur Village since 1890, increasingly known and respected. Around 1907, he was elevated as the Just King (Ratu Adil) with the title Prabu Pangeran Suryangalam.

## Arrest and Exile

The growth of the Samin people with their tax refusal movement made the colonial government worried about the Samin people. With advice from Snouck Hurgronje, advisor on Native and Islamic Affairs, the government arrested Samin Surosentiko along with five of his followers on November 8, 1907.

"This kind of movement is not dangerous according to his opinion because it can be quickly crushed by exiling its leaders or advocates," continued Marwati Djoened et al.

After being arrested, Samin and five of his followers, according to the newspaper *De Sumatra Post* dated March 3, 1908, were imprisoned in Semarang. The newspaper called Samin, who was then 48 years old, a revolutionary.

## Exile to Various Regions

Samin and his followers were then exiled to different areas. *Het Nieuws van den dag van Nederlandsch Indisch* dated December 24, 1907 reported that based on the Government Decision dated December 21, 1907, for the sake of peace and public order:

- **Samin, Singotirto alias Saridjam, and Kartogolo alias Sakidin** were exiled to the Padang Residency
- **Ronodikromo alias Randi and Soredjo alias Sadikin and Soerjani alias Sarpan** were exiled to Bengkulu
- **Soerowirjo alias Saroe and Tokromo alias Sakidin** were exiled to Manado

## Death in Exile

Samin never returned to his homeland. He died at his place of exile. The newspaper *Het Volk* edition September 11, 1933 stated that Samin died in the Padang Residency in 1914. Stories about Samin Surosentiko in his exile land in Sumatra were then no longer heard. The connection between Samin and his followers and family in Java was also severed.

"There might be traces in Sawahlunto. At Mbah Suro's mine, they say. I have never been there," said Sutejo, one of Samin Surosentiko's great-great-grandchildren who now lives in the Randublatung area, Blora.

## Mbah Suro and the Mine Shaft

Samin Surosentiko is often associated with Lubang Mbah Suro (Mbah Suro's Shaft) which is now a historical tourist site. Mbah Suro himself had connections to Sawahlunto, the Ombilin coal mining area in West Sumatra which employed many chain people, meaning prisoners whose feet were chained and the chains were tied to heavy iron. The tabloid *Mak Itam* 2013 edition stated that Mbah Suro came from Java.

"According to the tabloid, he taught Saminism, a heretical teaching of traditional Javanese farmers in Blora before being transferred to Sawahlunto in 1902, he was punished for opposing Dutch tax policies," wrote Mannek Budiman and Abidin Kusno in *Collective Memory, Marginality, and Spatial Politics in Urban Indonesia*.

Mbah Suro was respected by other prisoners there. Mbah Suro, who is considered to be Samin Surosentiko, was then buried in Sawahlunto after his death. Although the graves of chain people have no names on their headstones, there is a prisoner registration number on the headstone. There are plans to move Mbah Suro's grave to Java, his homeland.

## A Legacy That Grows Stronger

After Samin's departure to Sumatra and his death, the number of Samin followers actually increased. Samin followers still exist today. If in the past they were at odds with the government because they considered government people to be dishonest, now Samin people are protected like other Indonesian citizens.
