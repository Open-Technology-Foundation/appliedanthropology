# Understanding the Samin Tribe and Sikep Teachings That Troubled the Colonizers

## Origins and History

The Samin Tribe takes its name from Ki Samin Surosentiko, a community leader born in Poso Village, Blora Regency, Central Java in 1859. He was a descendant of Adipati Brotodiningrat and had the birth name Raden Kohar. His father, Raden Surowijoyo (Samin Sepuh), had already started a moral movement to oppose the Dutch.

In 1890, Raden Kohar changed his name to Samin Surosentiko and began spreading his father's teachings in Klopoduwur. His followers grew rapidly—from 722 people in 1903 to 5,000 people in 1907. The community spread across Bojonegoro and southern Blora. His followers elevated Samin as the "Just King" (Ratu Adil) with the title Prabu Panembahan Suryangalam.

## Resistance Against Dutch Colonizers

Samin Surosentiko was known by the Dutch as a "recidivist" for not complying with colonial rules. His resistance strategy included refusing to pay taxes and taking forest wood without permission—all done without violence. As a result, he was captured and exiled to Padang, North Sumatra along with eight of his followers, dying in exile in 1914.

## Sedulur Sikep Teachings

Saminism developed with the main principle of "Sedulur Sikep"—meaning "sibling" and "weapon" or non-violent resistance. The Samin community prefers to be called "Wong Sikep" (good and honest people). Other teachings include "Shoaling Ilat" (guarding one's speech) with a philosophy of doing good to others.

They adhere to the principle: "*Ono niro mergo ningsung, ono ningsung mergo niro*" (I exist because of you, you exist because of me), reflecting a commitment not to harm others.

## Contemporary Life and Culture

Most Samin community members work as farmers in the Kendeng Mountains. They are reluctant to trade because they worry about deceiving people. Many descendants do not pursue formal education, choosing to teach their children themselves so that their noble culture remains preserved.
