# The Anti-Colonial Resistance of the Samin Movement

## Key Facts

The Samin movement emerged in the late nineteenth century in Central Java as resistance against Dutch colonialism. This resistance predates Indonesian nationalist organizations, although they differ substantially from each other.

## Samin Surosentiko (1859-1914)

Born as Raden Kohar, he adopted the name Samin—the Javanese word for "the same"—because he believed that "all living beings were equal." Around 1890, he began preaching peaceful resistance, stating that followers did not need to pay taxes to the colonial system and could continue to take teak wood from state forests.

## Characteristics of the Movement

The movement had approximately 3,000 followers at its peak. Notably, Samin himself and his followers were illiterate—a direct consequence of colonial education policy. Because Saminists themselves left no texts, our knowledge is based entirely on Dutch colonial administrative documents.

### Features

- Approximately 3,000 followers at peak
- Illiteracy among leaders and followers
- Peaceful resistance without violence
- Refusal of tax payment
- Continuation of traditional forest use

## Colonial Response

Dutch authorities feared uprisings. Samin was arrested in 1907 during a selamatan (ritual feast) and later banished to Sawahlunto (West Sumatra), where he died in 1914. Dutch newspapers portrayed him as an anarchist and communist.

## Aftermath

Family members and followers continued the movement. A report from 1917 showed a decline from 3,000 to 2,305 followers.

## Contemporary Relevance

The movement, now called Sedulur Sikep, still exists in Indonesia. The heirs of Samin's philosophy remain active in resistance against industrial development that threatens their traditional way of life.
