# What is 'Indian Philosophy?' - Dharma Traditions, Dharma Studies, and the Quest to Interpret South Asian Religions for Global Audiences

**By Christopher Jain Miller, PhD**
*Vice President of Academic Affairs and Professor of Jain and Yoga Studies at Arihanta Institute*

## Overview

Indian philosophy encompasses diverse schools of thought addressing existence, rebirth, knowledge, morality, and reality. The concept of Dharma—central to these traditions—guides ethical behavior, social order, and spiritual development across Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism.

## Understanding Dharma

Dharma translates roughly as "duty," "righteousness," or "moral law," though its meaning varies across texts and contexts. According to the article, this term represents "universal principles that promote harmony and balance in society." The *Bhagavad Gītā* illustrates this through its emphasis on performing one's own responsibilities rather than mastering another's duties.

The author emphasizes an important caveat: while these texts present themselves as universal teachings, they emerged from specific historical, social, and political contexts. The *Gītā*, for instance, functioned partly to reinforce existing social hierarchies in its original setting.

## Dharma Traditions' Philosophical Foundations

**Hinduism** explores ultimate reality through schools like Vedānta, Sāṃkhya, and Nyāya, drawing from the Vedas, Upaniṣads, and epics.

**Buddhism** presents distinctive perspectives through the Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path, introducing *anātman* (no-self) to challenge established understandings of individual identity.

**Jainism** emphasizes *ahiṃsā* (non-violence) as central to Dharma, with detailed ethical and metaphysical discussions in foundational texts.

## Dharma Studies as Academic Field

Dharma Studies represents an interdisciplinary approach examining how Dharma manifests across South Asian religions. Scholars work to bridge traditional understandings with contemporary ethical challenges, including environmental sustainability and social justice.

However, Miller notes a significant gap: while scholars increasingly frame Dharma within modern issues, "the issues of caste discrimination, gender inequality, and the harms of dairy" remain largely unaddressed.

## Global Interpretation Challenges

Interpreting Dharma for diverse audiences requires cultural sensitivity and scholarly awareness of one's own social position. The challenge involves avoiding oversimplification while recognizing how political considerations shape academic discourse.

## Conclusion

Indian philosophy offers enduring insights into ethical living and responsibility. As Dharma Studies develops, scholars have opportunities to expand discussions beyond traditional frameworks to address previously avoided social justice dimensions.
