# Morality and Evolutionary Biology

**Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy**

## Overview

This comprehensive article examines the intersection of evolutionary biology and ethics, exploring three major analytical approaches: descriptive evolutionary ethics (explaining moral capacities scientifically), prescriptive evolutionary ethics (deriving moral guidance from evolution), and evolutionary metaethics (examining implications for moral truth and knowledge).

## Key Distinctions

### Three Kinds of Evolutionary Ethics Appeal

The article identifies distinct projects:

1. **Descriptive Evolutionary Ethics**: Uses evolutionary theory to explain origins of human moral capacities, emotional responses, and behavioral patterns through natural selection.

2. **Prescriptive Evolutionary Ethics**: Attempts to justify normative ethical claims based on evolutionary facts—a project fraught with philosophical difficulties.

3. **Evolutionary Metaethics**: Employs evolutionary theory to address fundamental questions about whether objective moral truths exist and whether we can know them.

### Empirical vs. Normative Morality

A critical distinction separates two senses of "morality":

- **Empirical sense**: Observable moral phenomena—actual moral judgments, emotions, and behaviors studied scientifically
- **Normative sense**: Standards for how we *ought* to live; the proper subject of philosophical ethics

## Descriptive Evolutionary Ethics

### The Capacity for Moral Judgment

The article explains that human moral capacity likely evolved through natural selection because it enhanced social cohesion and cooperation. Kitcher's three-stage model proposes:

1. Evolution of psychological altruism in coalition contexts
2. Development of normative guidance capacities with reactive emotions (guilt, resentment)
3. Cultural evolution producing sophisticated moral systems

### The Autonomy Question

A central tension emerges: while evolutionary forces shaped our general cognitive capacities, this doesn't necessarily explain specific moral *content*. The "autonomy assumption" suggests humans can engage in genuine moral reasoning independent of evolutionary conditioning.

The article notes: "people have, to greater or lesser degrees, a capacity for reasoning that follows *autonomous standards* appropriate to the subjects in question" rather than merely serving evolved instincts.

### Post Hoc Rationalization Challenge

Researchers like Jonathan Haidt argue many moral judgments result from emotional reactions that people subsequently rationalize rather than from genuine reasoning. However, the article identifies important counterarguments:

- Some moral beliefs (concerning equality, cosmopolitan obligations) seem difficult to explain through evolutionary emotional conditioning alone
- Even emotionally-triggered intuitions may reflect prior moral reflection and learning
- The breadth and variation of human moral systems suggests significant autonomous input

## Prescriptive Evolutionary Ethics

### The Naturalistic Fallacy Problem

A fundamental obstacle undermines attempts to derive moral conclusions from evolutionary facts: knowing a trait evolved through natural selection provides no warrant for endorsing it morally. The article emphasizes this autonomy principle: "the question for a rational agent is always: is it right for me to exercise it, or should I instead renounce and resist it?"

### Corrective Uses

Evolutionary biology can play corrective roles in ethics more modestly:

- Undermining moral views that rely on false creationist assumptions
- Exposing how psychological biases distort moral judgment
- Revealing human susceptibilities to manipulation and moral regression

### Moral Progress and Adaptive Plasticity

Buchanan and Powell argue humans possess "adaptive plasticity" in moral psychology—we contain *both* tribalistic and inclusivist dispositions, activated by different environmental conditions. This suggests moral progress toward greater inclusivity remains possible without requiring genetic modification, though it demands sustained institutional effort.

## Evolutionary Metaethics

### Debunking Arguments

Some philosophers argue evolutionary explanations of moral belief undermine moral realism—the view that objective moral truths exist independently of our attitudes. Sharon Street's debunking argument contends natural selection shaped our moral beliefs based on fitness effects, not truth-tracking capacity. Therefore, our moral beliefs "would be a sheer coincidence" if they tracked independent moral facts.

### Realist Responses

The article identifies several realist counterarguments:

1. **Autonomous reflection model**: Many moral beliefs result from genuine reasoning about moral facts, not merely evolutionary conditioning

2. **Moral facts and fitness**: On some naturalistic theories, moral truths concern social stability—precisely what evolution shaped us to pursue

3. **Burden of proof**: Debunkers must establish that moral beliefs are *pervasively saturated* with evolutionary influence, not merely influenced by it

4. **Conceptual refinement**: Even if moral concepts originated evolutionarily, refined conceptions can track genuine truths

## Critical Implications

The research suggests:

- **No simple moves from science to ethics**: Evolutionary facts don't straightforwardly justify or undermine moral positions
- **Pluralistic explanation needed**: Different moral beliefs likely require different explanatory models
- **Philosophical foundations matter**: Metaethical assumptions shape how we interpret evolutionary evidence
- **Human dignity in question**: Our special moral status cannot rely on outdated creationist biology, yet our distinctive capacities may ground alternative justifications

## Conclusion

Rather than allowing evolutionary biology to "invade" moral philosophy or dismissing biological insights, the most productive approach integrates scientific findings with philosophical analysis. Progress requires acknowledging both how evolution shaped our moral capacities and how human reasoning can transcend those origins through autonomous reflection on genuinely moral considerations.
