# Cultural Evolution

**Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy**
*First published Sun Dec 23, 2007; substantive revision Mon May 22, 2023*

## Overview

Cultural evolution research examines how humans and other organisms learn from species members, exploring both the origins of social learning capacities and their effects on population adaptation over time. This field bridges cognitive science, anthropology, and evolutionary biology.

## Core Concepts

### What Evolves?

Researchers define culture as "information capable of affecting individuals' behavior that they acquire from other members of their species through teaching, imitation, and other forms of social learning." However, the culture concept remains contested across disciplines, with variation in how theorists understand its levels (individual vs. group) and roles (medium, background conditions, or adaptive strategies).

### Transmission and Inheritance

Two major approaches distinguish cultural transmission:

1. **Content-based**: Some forms emphasize symbolic systems (like language) that enable rapid recombination and modification of information.

2. **Channel-based**: Others focus on social learning mechanisms—situations where "an individual's learning is influenced by others"—as distinctive pathways separate from genetic or environmental transmission.

The boundary between social and individual learning remains blurry, since most environments reflect prior human activity.

### Reproduction and Selection

Disagreement persists regarding selection and reproduction in culture. While some theorists argue strict biological analogies don't apply, others employ frameworks ranging from populational (frequency changes over time) to selectional (appealing to natural selection) to replicator approaches (assuming cultural replicators function like genes).

## Major Theoretical Approaches

### Memetics

Richard Dawkins proposed that "memes"—cultural replicators including ideas, techniques, and fashions—spread through populations by making copies of themselves. However, critics identify serious problems:

- **Replication concerns**: Cultural transmission is often error-prone and reconstructive rather than faithful copying
- **Lineage problems**: Ideas rarely trace back to single sources; individuals acquire beliefs from multiple community members
- **Atomization issues**: Cultural traits stand in logical relations, making discrete unit identification difficult

### Dual-Inheritance Theory

This more influential approach integrates cultural inheritance into evolutionary models without assuming cultural transmission mirrors genetic inheritance. Researchers document empirically-supported learning biases (like prestige bias and conformist bias) and model population-level consequences.

Key insight: Conformist bias can maintain population-level distributions despite error-prone individual learning, enabling cumulative cultural evolution without requiring faithful replication.

### Cultural Attraction Theory

Dan Sperber's approach emphasizes "attractors"—stable outcomes of reconstructive learning processes shaped by shared psychological dispositions, cognitive biases, or ecological constraints. This framework explains cultural recurrence without literal copying and accounts for systematic transformations in cultural traits.

### Cultural Systems Approach

Recent work examines sequential trait acquisition and compatibility relationships between already-acquired traits. This approach formalizes humanities and social science claims about culture using systems thinking rather than explicitly evolutionary frameworks.

## Extensions and Elaborations

### Cumulative Culture

Theorists investigate how adaptive cultural traits accumulate over generations through ratchet-like processes where modifications are retained and built upon. Examples include manioc processing techniques requiring knowledge unlikely discovered by individuals alone.

Challenges include defining what constitutes "incremental improvement" and identifying cumulative culture in non-human species, though evidence for such accumulation in primates remains contentious.

### Cultural Group Selection

Some theorists argue groups differing in culturally inherited traits compete effectively, potentially favoring within-group cooperation and altruism. However, distinguishing genuine group-level selection from individual-level selection mediated through learning remains conceptually challenging.

### Evolvability

Following Kim Sterelny's work, researchers examine conditions enabling cumulative cultural adaptation: stable environmental pressures, development permitting trait modification without disrupting other functions, diverse available variation, and mechanisms suppressing "outlaws" that undermine group coordination.

### Macroevolution

Phylogenetic methods borrowed from biology help reconstruct cultural genealogies, though cultural change involves more reticulation than simple branching trees. Archaeological databases increasingly enable identifying cultural evolutionary patterns, diversification rates, and extinction events.

## Persisting Concerns

Critics worry that general cultural evolutionary theories lack explanatory power beyond restating psychological or historical mechanisms. As Elliott Sober noted, unless theories explain *why* some ideas spread better than others, they merely relabel familiar social scientific explanations using evolutionary vocabulary.

Defenders respond that population-thinking models reveal non-obvious consequences of individual learning dispositions and that evolutionary frameworks can explain unconscious cultural selection—processes not transparent to conscious evaluation.

## Conclusion

Rather than seeking grand theories unifying social science around natural selection, contemporary cultural evolution pursues eclectic research questions: How widespread are learning abilities? Why do species differ in learning capacities? How does learning interact with genetic evolution? What conditions enable cumulative improvements or other-regarding behavior?

This approach freely collaborates with successful social science research while adapting explanatory models from population genetics, epidemiology, and economics, avoiding commitment to any single theoretical framework.
