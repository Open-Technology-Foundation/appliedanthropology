# Dharma

Dharma is a key concept in Indian religions with multiple meanings across Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism. The earliest mention of the term dharma is in the Rigveda, a sacred Hindu text written in about 1500 BCE.

## Etymology

Etymologically, dharma comes from the Sanskrit *dhr-*, meaning "to hold" or "to support," thus referring to the law that sustains things—from one's life to society, and to the Universe at large.

- **Sanskrit**: धर्म (*dhárma*) - "that which upholds or supports"
- **Pali/Prakrit**: *dhamma*
- **Semantic parallel**: Greek *themis* ("fixed decree, statute, law")

In Classical Sanskrit and Vedic Sanskrit, the stem is thematic: *dhárma-*.

## In Hinduism

In Hinduism, dharma denotes behavior that is considered to be in accord with Ṛta—the "order and custom" that makes life and universe possible. This includes:

- Duties
- Rights
- Laws
- Conduct
- Virtues
- Ethics to form the "righteous way of living"

### Universal and Specific Dharma

Hindu philosophy recognizes two levels of dharma:

- **Universal dharma** (*sadharana dharma*): Applies to all people and includes virtues such as truthfulness, non-injury, and generosity
- **Specific dharma** (*svadharma*): Varies based on an individual's class, status, and life circumstances

### Organizing Principle

Dharma is an organizing principle in Hinduism that applies to:

- Human beings in solitude
- Human interaction with other humans and nature
- Inanimate objects
- All of cosmos and its parts

In ancient myths as well as texts of Hinduism, Dharma meant cosmic law, rules that created the Universe from chaos.

### Legal Foundations

Dharma serves as the foundation for Hindu legal and ethical texts:

- **Dharma-sutras**: Ancient religious manuals considered the earliest sources of Hindu law
- **Dharma-shastra**: More extensive compilations that developed later

## In Buddhism

In Buddhism, dharma (Pali: *dhamma*) has several meanings:

### The Doctrine

Dharma refers to the teachings of the Buddha and the universal truth common to all individuals at all times. In Buddhist practice, dharma forms part of the **Triratna** ("Three Jewels"):

1. The Buddha
2. The Dharma (doctrine)
3. The Sangha (community of believers)

Together these constitute the foundation for Buddhist refuge and practice.

### True Nature of Reality

Dharma also refers to the true nature of reality. In Buddhist philosophy, dhamma/dharma is the term for specific "phenomena" and for the ultimate truth.

### Dharmas (Plural)

In Buddhist metaphysics, the plural form "dharmas" refers to the interdependent elements composing the empirical world.

### Cosmic Law

Buddhism held the Hindu view of Dharma as "cosmic law," as in the working of Karma.

## In Jainism

Dharma in Jainism has multiple meanings:

### Moral Teaching

Dharma refers to the teachings of Tirthankara (Jina) and the body of doctrine pertaining to purification and moral transformation.

### Nonviolence

According to Jain texts, nonviolence is regarded as the most excellent Dharma, and no religion can be compared to the religion of nonviolence.

### Unique Ontological Meaning

The use of the term *dharmāstikāya* to mean motion and to refer to an ontological sub-category is peculiar to Jainism. Jain philosophy incorporates dharma as moral virtue while adding a unique interpretation: dharma as an eternal "substance" (*dravya*) that serves as the medium enabling beings to move and function.

## Cosmic Law Concept

In Indian religion, dharma represents the eternal law of the cosmos, inherent in the very nature of things. In Indian tradition, the concept of **ṛta** (cosmic order) gave rise to the idea of dharma.

The term dharma here does not mean mere religion; it stands for:

- Duty
- Obligation
- Righteousness

In Hinduism, dharma is seen as the cosmic law both upheld by the gods and expressed in right behavior by humans, including adherence to the social order.
