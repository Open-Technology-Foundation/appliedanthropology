# Evolutionary Ethics

**By Doris Schroeder, Lancaster University**
*Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*

## Introduction

Evolutionary ethics attempts to connect philosophy with natural sciences by proposing that natural selection has equipped humans with moral inclinations. Rather than viewing morality as divinely inspired or purely rational, this approach suggests it emerged as an adaptive trait enhancing survival. Edward O. Wilson famously advocated for "biologicizing" ethics, arguing that scientists and philosophers should collaborate to understand morality through an evolutionary lens. The central challenge involves defining goodness using evolutionary principles and explaining why humans should act morally.

## 1. Key Figures and Key Concepts

### A. Charles Darwin

Charles Darwin initiated the biologization of ethics in *The Descent of Man* (1871). Confronting the puzzle of human moral sophistication, Darwin proposed morality evolved through two stages:

**First Stage - Social Instincts:** Darwin traced morality's roots to social instincts, mechanisms that facilitate group cohesion and parental care. These instincts emerged when animals began nurturing offspring, requiring cooperative capacities absent in earlier species. Such sociability enabled aggression to be directed outward against rival groups rather than within communities, providing survival advantages.

**Second Stage - Intellectual Reflection:** Evolved intellectual capacities allowed humans to reflect on actions and develop conscience—"the supreme judge and monitor" of behavior. This capacity, combined with social instincts, naturally led humans toward utilitarian principles valuing greatest happiness.

**Darwin's Ethical Framework:** Darwin answered the two fundamental ethical questions:

1. **Good vs. Evil:** Darwin adopted hedonistic utilitarianism, defining good as actions increasing overall happiness and reducing suffering.

2. **Why Be Moral?:** Humans possess biological inclinations toward sympathy and altruism because these traits proved advantageous in evolutionary competition.

### B. Herbert Spencer

Herbert Spencer (1820-1903) became evolutionary ethics' most passionate defender and developed Social Darwinism. His three-step theory proceeded as follows:

**Step One:** Spencer accepted hedonistic utilitarianism, viewing pleasure-seeking and pain-avoidance as directing all human behavior.

**Step Two:** He recognized two distinct impulse categories—self-regarding (personal satisfaction) and other-regarding (benefiting others)—both producing pleasure.

**Step Three:** Human cooperation requires balancing egoistic and altruistic traits through equity principles.

**Spencer's Controversial Legacy:** Spencer infamously advocated for non-intervention regarding suffering populations, suggesting assistance to "the bad" would "provide for our descendants a multitude of enemies." His philosophy justified social hierarchies and opposition to welfare, later becoming associated with abusive regimes like Nazism. Though popular in 19th-century North America, his reputation declined significantly during the 20th century.

### C. The Is-Ought Problem

David Hume (1711-1776) identified a persistent problem in moral systems: the imperceptible transition from describing what *is* to prescribing what *ought* to be. Hume observed that moral philosophers describing facts suddenly introduce normative claims without logical justification.

Hume's logical point: empirical facts cannot contain normative elements, so normative conclusions cannot validly follow from purely factual premises. Deductive validity requires all necessary information present in premises.

### D. The Naturalistic Fallacy

G.E. Moore (1873-1958) presented an independent but related challenge in *Principia Ethica* (1903). Moore distinguished between simple properties (indefinable, like "yellow") and complex properties (definable through constituent parts, like "colored").

**Moore's Core Claim:** "Good" constitutes a simple, indefinable property. Attempting to define goodness using other natural (empirically verifiable) properties commits the naturalistic fallacy.

### E. Sociobiology

Despite persistent philosophical challenges, evolutionary ethics advanced through sociobiology. In 1975, Edward O. Wilson's *Sociobiology: The New Synthesis* catalyzed the field by defining it as "the systematic study of the biological basis of all social behavior."

**Wilson's Approach:** Wilson proposed that ethical sentiments—hate, love, guilt, fear—emerge from the hypothalamus and limbic system, structures shaped by natural selection. Moral capacities evolved because they enhanced group survival.

## 2. Placement in Contemporary Ethical Theory

**Descriptive Ethics:** Examines moral beliefs across cultures and explains their origins. Nearly all societies prohibit incest; evolutionary theory suggests this taboo provides survival advantages. This remains evolutionary ethics' most promising domain.

**Normative Ethics:** Attempts to establish standards for judging actions. Evolutionary ethics might propose: "Actions increasing long-term evolutionary survival capacity are good; those decreasing it are bad." However, this framework remains underdeveloped.

**Metaethics:** Investigates morality's foundations. Michael Ruse argues morality constitutes "a collective illusion of the genes." We need to believe morality exists, so our biology ensures we do.

## 3. Challenges for Evolutionary Ethics

**Supererogatory Actions:** How can biology explain extraordinary self-sacrifice exceeding reciprocal altruism or enlightened self-interest?

**Transcendence of Biology:** Have humans moved beyond biological constraints, formulating goals regarding "goodness, beauty, and truth" that "have nothing to do directly with survival"?

**Universality Paradox:** Morality claims universality—"do not kill" applies regardless of relationship. Yet biologically useful altruism prioritizes family and group.

**Hume's Is-Ought Challenge:** The gap between natural scientific findings and moral prescriptions persists unresolved.

**Moore's Naturalistic Fallacy:** Despite decades since *Principia Ethica*, defining goodness through natural properties remains philosophically problematic.

## Conclusion

Evolutionary ethics represents a relatively recent philosophical approach emerging from contemporary interdisciplinary collaboration. While such cooperation between scientists and philosophers generates valuable insights, evolutionary ethics remains developmentally early, facing formidable conceptual hurdles requiring substantial additional work.
