# Evolutionary Ethics

**By Michael Klenk**
*From: Introduction to Philosophy: Ethics (Rebus Community)*

## Introduction

The chapter opens with Edward O. Wilson's controversial 1975 book *Sociobiology*, which extended evolutionary explanations to human moral behavior. Wilson proposed that ethics should be studied using scientific methods—a naturalistic approach. His suggestion generated significant backlash, though modern evolutionary ethics has become a legitimate research program.

## Two Forms of Naturalism in Ethics

**Methodological Ethical Naturalism:** An approach to *how* we study ethics, incorporating findings from psychology, anthropology, biology, and other sciences alongside traditional philosophical analysis.

**Metaphysical Ethical Naturalism:** A position about *what exists*—the view that moral facts exist and can be described in natural terms.

The chapter emphasizes that one can adopt methodological naturalism without committing to metaphysical naturalism.

## Evolutionary Theory Basics

Natural selection operates through differential survival and reproduction based on heritable traits. Organisms better adapted to their environment have greater fitness and pass advantageous genes to offspring. This mechanism provides the foundation for evolutionary ethics.

## Four Central Questions

### 1. What Should We Do?

Early proponents like Herbert Spencer attempted to derive moral principles directly from evolution. This approach faltered due to:
- *Hume's Law:* The prohibition against deriving "ought" from "is"
- *Moore's Challenge:* The "naturalistic fallacy" of defining "good" in natural terms

Contemporary evolutionary ethics takes a more modest approach, identifying where moral rules apply rather than establishing the rules themselves.

### 2. Why Are We Moral?

Humans display remarkable prosocial behavior compared to other primates. Evolutionary explanations propose morality as a cooperation mechanism:
- *Gene's-eye perspective:* Kin selection and reciprocal altruism explain moral behavior through genetic self-interest
- *Group selection:* Competition between groups selected for cooperative individuals with moral psychology

Cultural evolution also plays a role, with transmitted norms shaping both genetic and behavioral evolution.

### 3. Are There Moral Facts?

Philosophers Michael Ruse and Sharon Street argue that evolutionary explanations suggest moral facts may not exist. Their reasoning: if evolution fully explains moral beliefs, moral facts become "explanatorily redundant"—we need not posit their existence.

### 4. Can We Have Justified Moral Beliefs?

Street's evolutionary argument raises epistemological concerns: if our moral beliefs result from evolutionary pressures rather than moral facts, they may be unjustified because they're insensitive to actual moral truths.

## Key Concepts

- **Phenotype:** Observable characteristics shaped by evolution
- **Fitness:** An organism's reproductive success
- **Reciprocal altruism:** Mutual benefit from helping unrelated individuals
- **Group selection:** Natural selection operating at the group level

## Important Historical Case Study

The "Social Darwinism" box details the *Buck v. Bell* (1927) case, illustrating how evolutionary theory was misused to justify eugenics programs—a cautionary lesson about deriving moral conclusions from evolutionary facts.

## Conclusion

While evolutionary ethics cannot definitively answer "what we ought to do," the field illuminates the origins of human moral capacities and raises profound questions about moral knowledge, facts, and the scope of naturalistic inquiry in ethics.
