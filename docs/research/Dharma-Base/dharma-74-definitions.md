# Dharma: 74 Definitions Across Religious Traditions

## Overview

Dharma (धर्म) is a Sanskrit term with profound significance across multiple spiritual and philosophical traditions. The word derives from the root *dhṛ*, meaning "to sustain, uphold, protect," with the affix *man*.

## In Hinduism

### Shaivism
In Shaivism, dharma represents "virtue" accomplished through spiritual practices. The *Kakṣapuṭatantra* describes dharma as one of four primary goals alongside wealth, love, and liberation, cultivated through mantra recitation using lotus seed rosaries.

### Dharmashastra (Religious Law)
"That which sustains and protects creation in a disciplined manner," according to legal texts. The *Manusamhitā* defines it as conduct followed by virtuous, Veda-learned individuals. Five categories exist:

- Varṇa-dharma (caste duties)
- Āśrama-dharma (life-stage duties)
- Varṇāśrama-dharma (combined duties)
- Naimittika-dharma (occasional duties)
- Guṇa-dharma (qualification-based duties)

### Puranas
In mythological texts, Dharma appears as a deity born from Brahma's conception, serving as the means for achieving all goals. As a personified force, Dharma manifested as a four-footed bull and took the form of Manu.

### Vaisheshika Philosophy
Dharma functions as "merit," one of the qualities (*guṇas*) classified as a metaphysical category (*padārtha*) that explains universal nature and existence.

### Mimamsa Philosophy
"That which leads to the highest common good and is distinguished by Vedic injunctions." Jaimini's definition: *codaṇā-lakṣaṇaḥ arthaḥ dharmaḥ*. It cannot be known empirically but requires Vedic sources.

### Vyakarana (Grammar)
A property qualifying things, letters, or words; also represents traditional practices (*ṛṣisaṃpradāya*) laid down by sages for posterity.

### Jyotisha (Astronomy/Astrology)
"Charity" and the Hindu doctrine of sacred law representing moral and religious duties prescribed for humans.

### Pancaratra (Vaishnavism)
Righteousness manifested through hand gestures (*mudrā*) in worship; represents the "all-pervading, spotless" principle without beginning or destruction, bearing symbols of desire and wealth.

### Arthashastra (Politics/Welfare)
The concept evolved from cosmic order (*ṛta*) into duty, obligation, and righteousness. It represents "the whole way of life in which ethical values are supreme."

### Kavya (Poetry)
In the *Kathāsaritsāgara*, Dharma appears as an ancient king of the Deccan, "chief of virtuous men" despite relatives aspiring to replace him.

### Nyaya Philosophy
"Merit" constituting *adṛṣṭa* (destiny) alongside demerit. It produces happiness and liberation through consciousness contact with the inner organ, conditioned by pure thoughts.

### Vaishnavism
"That which sustains"; the socio-religious duties prescribed in scriptures for different castes and life-stages within the *varṇāśrama* system.

### Ayurveda
Righteousness, justice, duty, and obligations. One of four *puruṣārthas* (life goals), inferred from good conduct yielding heaven and happiness.

### Yoga
"Meritorious actions" whose cessation allows duality to disappear, enabling the mind to attain non-dualistic states.

### Vedanta
Mental concepts to be discarded by liberation-seekers; also "religion" which doesn't affect true yogis established in self-knowledge.

### Kamashastra
One of the three pursuits (*trivarga*) of life—dharma, wealth, and desire—which should harmonize without clash across one's hundred-year lifespan.

### General Hindu Definition
"The code of life" upon which Hinduism is based. The ultimate objective involves uniting with the Supreme Being (*Paramātman*) through practicing dharma's principles.

## In Buddhism

### Mahayana
Buddhist interpretations identify three types of dharma:

1. Supreme dharma (nirvāṇa)
2. Means of attaining nirvāṇa (Noble Eightfold Path)
3. Good and truthful words promoting the Path

"Phenomena" that are empty (*śūnya*) of inherent nature due to dependent co-arising, yet emptiness itself possesses no independent existence.

### Theravada
Ultimate components of existence; momentary events composing experience. Four categories: consciousness (*citta*), mental factors (*cetasika*—52 types), physical form (*rūpa*—28 types), and extinction (*nibbāna*—unconditioned).

### Tibetan Buddhism
"The activity field of mental objects" (*dharmāyatana*), representing one of twelve activity fields. Also denotes the Buddha's teachings or particular teaching sets (e.g., Six Dharmas of Nāropa).

### General Buddhist Definition
Central Buddhist notion with multiple meanings: cosmic law governing rebirth; Buddha's teachings expressing universal truth; behavioral norms and ethics; manifestations of reality; mental content reflecting things in consciousness.

## In Jainism

### General Definition
Multiple meanings depending on context:

1. **Fifteenth Tīrthaṅkara (Dharmanātha)**: Red-colored spiritual teacher, 45 bows tall, symbolized by a Vajra, whose parents were Bhānu and Suvratā

2. **True Religion**: Represented by ten characteristics (*dasalakṣaṇa*) including non-violence and non-pride

3. **Medium of Motion**: Assists movement of objects, contrasting with *adharma* (medium of rest)

4. **Virtuous Meditation**: One of four meditation types supporting spiritual purification

## In India History & Geography

Dharma appears in epigraphical records as "sacred law, religious merit, meritorious gift, pious work, charity, moral precept," and "virtue or religious merit." It also designates gotras (clans) among South Indian tribes and represents one of four Siddhas in Tantric Buddhist traditions.

## Language Dictionaries

### Marathi
Religious practice, customary caste observances, system of worship, Vedic precepts, alms-giving, virtue, nature, character, and prescribed duties varying by social role.

### Sanskrit
Twenty-three primary meanings: religion, law, usage, practice, custom, merit, virtue, righteousness, duty, right, justice, piety, morality, nature, disposition, characteristic property, manner, sacrifice, good company, devotion, and proper conduct.

### Hindi/Kannada/Nepali
Consistent usage emphasizing righteousness, duty, religious conduct, virtue, justice, and moral law governing individual and social behavior.
