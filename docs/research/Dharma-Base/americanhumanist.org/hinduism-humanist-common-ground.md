# Humanist Common Ground: Hinduism

## A Brief Overview of the Development of Hinduism

Hinduism's origins trace back more than four thousand years to pre-historic India, making it "the oldest living major religion." Unlike religions founded by specific individuals, Hinduism emerged as a set of rules and regulations promoting disciplined living, emphasizing duties, moral values, and self-realization through meditation.

The tradition is polytheistic, featuring innumerable deities representing philosophies, natural forces, and moral qualities. Key sacred texts include the four Vedas, Upanishads, and Puranas—compilations addressing rituals, hymns, and daily practices rather than revelations from a single founder.

A simplified timeline shows:
- 3,000–1500 BCE: Indus Valley Civilization
- 1500–500 BCE: Vedic Period
- 500 CE–1200 CE: Theological establishment of Vedanta
- 1757–1947 CE: British Period and reform movements
- 1947 CE–present: Hinduism as a world religion

Neo-Hinduism describes thinkers willing to reinterpret traditional philosophies in light of new circumstances while maintaining commitment to practical human service.

## Key Principles, Beliefs and Concepts

**Dharma**: All humans possess inherent duties and obligations. Fulfilling dharma benefits individuals and society, representing righteous conduct and moral law.

**Karma**: Past actions influence present circumstances. Connected to reincarnation beliefs, positive thoughts and deeds generate positive results.

**Moksha**: Liberation occurs when the soul unites with Brahman, transcending the cycle of death and rebirth.

**Brahman**: Hinduism's exclusive conception of ultimate reality—not anthropomorphic but "the transcendent origin of all things."

**Yamas and Niyamas**: Essential moral codes emphasizing nonviolence, truthfulness, honesty, compassion, modesty, and generosity.

Scholars note that "Hinduism gives less importance to God's image than to values one carries and individual evolution," making it among the world's most secular religions.

## The Key Principles of Humanism

Humanism represents "a non-religious ethical outlook based on human affairs," grounded in human experience rather than supernatural doctrine. It emphasizes:

- Personal and collective responsibility for ethical living
- Human dignity and intrinsic worth
- Cooperation for common good
- Reasoned approaches to contemporary challenges
- Progressive advancement toward ideals through human effort alone

From the Humanist Manifestos:

**Manifesto I (1933)** stressed replacing worship with "heightened personal life and cooperative social well-being."

**Manifesto II (1973)** described humanism as "an ethical process" transcending "divisive particulars" and "dogmatic creeds."

**Manifesto III (2003)** emphasized human responsibility: "The responsibility for our lives...is ours and ours alone."

## Comparisons and Contrasts: On Common Ground

Both traditions prioritize values and personal growth over doctrinal belief. M.J. Akbar observed that "Hinduism is synonymous with humanism. That is its essence."

**Key Shared Values:**
- Emphasis on ethical conduct and moral responsibility
- Focus on present-moment living and personal development
- Tolerance for diverse perspectives
- Commitment to human welfare
- Freedom of thought and belief

**Notable Differences:**
Hinduism traditionally embraces supernatural elements—an afterlife, reincarnation, and karma as mystical forces. Humanism, by contrast, rejects supernaturalism, asserting that "when a person dies there can be no further existence."

However, karma can be reinterpreted rationally: positive actions naturally encourage reciprocal kindness, creating feedback loops rather than cosmic balancing.

Both traditions embrace the Golden Rule. In Hindu texts: "This is the sum of duty; do naught onto others what you would not have them do unto you."

## Hindu Nontheism

A 2008 Pew Forum study revealed significant nontheistic dimensions within Hinduism: 53% of American Hindus view "God as an Impersonal Force," while 5% reject belief entirely—potentially 550,000 and 30,000 individuals respectively.

Dr. Jay Lakhani notes: "Nontheistic Hinduism does not require a God to prescribe morality."

## We Are on The Same Page

**From Hindu Perspectives:**

Mahatma Gandhi: "Search after truth through non-violent means...Hinduism is a relentless pursuit after truth."

Swami Vivekananda: "We are responsible for what we are...whatever we wish to be...we have the power to make ourselves."

**From Humanist Perspectives:**

Ron Herman: "It is our common humanism that defines our morality."

Paul Kurtz: "Our moral obligations...encompass the entire globe and every person on it."

## Conclusion

Both Hinduism and humanism share fundamental commitments to duty, ethics, and human development as life paths. Whether framed as dharma or obligations, these traditions converge on principles emphasizing personal responsibility, compassionate action, and societal flourishing through reasoned, ethical living.

Many Hindus discover alignment with humanist values, and dual identification remains entirely compatible.
