# Ethics / Morality

**By James Laidlaw**
*William Wyse Professor of Social Anthropology, University of Cambridge*
*Open Encyclopedia of Anthropology*

## Abstract

The anthropology of ethics represents both a continuous concern within the discipline and a radical new departure. This entry explains how moral life has been traditionally understood in anthropology, why existing approaches came to seem insufficient, and the intellectual traditions now informing new directions. Key influences include virtue ethics, ordinary language philosophy, Michel Foucault's genealogy of ethics, phenomenology, and experimental moral psychology.

## Introduction

A fundamental paradox characterizes the field: anthropology has always engaged with morality, yet the anthropology of ethics is genuinely novel. Early influential anthropologists like Westermarck and Marett placed moral variation at the discipline's center. However, the current "ethical turn" represents something distinctly different from this earlier engagement.

## Historical Approaches to Morality

### Durkheimian Influence

Émile Durkheim profoundly shaped anthropological thinking about morality. He proposed replacing philosophical speculation with a positivist science of "moral facts." For Durkheim, morality consisted of socially-sanctioned rules that regulated individual desires in service to social order. He argued that "rules of good behaviour" required sacred authority to ensure willing compliance.

Durkheim's influence established a streamlined view of ethical life. As one critic notes, it eliminated "any philosophical perplexity about human freedom" and removed tragic conflicts from ethical consideration. Morality became merely "that mechanical process whereby individuals become a functioning 'part' of society."

### Boasian Anthropology

American anthropology, less influenced by Durkheim, treated morality as culturally distinctive approved ways of thinking and acting. The central problem remained explaining how "enculturation" processes made individuals embody cultural values.

### Marxist Variations

Marxist anthropologists identified how dominant ideologies induced subordinated groups to follow rules against their interests. Some emphasized working-class resistance and alternative moral economies challenging dominant systems.

## Limitations of Traditional Approaches

Mid-twentieth-century anthropology produced rich ethnographic descriptions of morality within kinship, economic, political, and religious domains. Yet these studies largely bypassed fundamental ethical questions: How do people doubt dominant values? What happens when facing irreconcilable value conflicts? Can personal responsibility and freedom be reduced to social causation?

Despite periodic calls for dialogue with moral philosophy—by thinkers ranging from Raymond Firth to Edith and May Edel—sustained engagement never developed. Philosophers dismissed anthropology as merely representing cultural relativism, precluding substantive conversation.

## The Contemporary Anthropology of Ethics

### Emergence and Growth

Around 2000, sustained interest in ethics emerged, liberated from narrow concerns with social control and enculturation. Three programmatic essays by Michael Lambek, James Faubion, and James Laidlaw independently advocated for serious engagement with ethical life. Subsequent workshops, monographs, and introductory readers established the field's trajectory.

### Two Salient Features

Contemporary work exhibits: first, systematic engagement with intellectual traditions offering sustained ethical reflection; and second, emerging debates addressing general theoretical matters and substantive controversies about ethical change in specific societies.

## Major Intellectual Traditions

### Virtue Ethics

Virtue ethics emerged from reaction against abstract, deductive moral philosophy. Beginning with G.E.M. Anscombe's 1958 essay, this tradition emphasizes "careful description of linguistic categories, especially those describing aspects of character, conduct, and social relations."

Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* directly inspired anthropological work. Talal Asad applied MacIntyre's framework to Islamic anthropology, influencing Saba Mahmood's and Charles Hirschkind's influential ethnographies of piety movements. Michael Lambek and Cheryl Mattingly developed virtue-ethical analytical methods for anthropology, exemplifying how virtues constitute frameworks for flourishing within historically-located ethical systems.

### Ordinary Language Philosophy

This tradition, descended from Ludwig Wittgenstein, emphasizes how action and concepts intertwine. Michael Lambek and Veena Das argue that "ethics is immanent in human action as such." Das particularly insists the ethical belongs to the ordinary everyday, not to extraordinary transcendent goods claimed by institutions.

This position attracts support but generates debate. Critics question whether "immanent ethics" risks collapsing ethics into "the social" and whether treating ordinary and extraordinary categories normatively forecloses ethnographic understanding.

### Michel Foucault's Genealogy of Ethics

Foucault's later work traces how "the desiring subject" emerged in modernity. He contrasts modern "hermeneutics of desire"—discovering oneself through hidden desires—with classical Athens's "ethics of existence," where citizens consciously fashioned themselves for exercising freedom and power.

Foucault develops conceptual resources distinguishing moral systems dominated by rules from those organized around "optative projects of ethical self-fashioning." His analytics have enabled productive ethnographic studies, though anthropologists express "nervousness" about his emphasis on freedom, fearing potential ethnocentrism.

### Phenomenology

Phenomenological traditions emphasizing structures of experience and consciousness have long influenced anthropology. Recent anthropologists propose phenomenological approaches to ethics, though they draw on divergent foundational thinkers—Merleau-Ponty, Heidegger, and others—implying different trajectories.

Some suggest phenomenology best understood as one "theoretical vocabulary" among others rather than unified research programme. Nevertheless, phenomenological concepts productively inform ethnographies of varying character.

### Experimental Psychology

Experimental moral psychology research examines how people make moral judgments, particularly emphasizing emotions' roles. Anthropologists generally find methodology unrealistic and assumptions parochial, yet pioneering work by Rita Astuti and Maurice Bloch demonstrates possibilities.

Richard Shweder's "cultural psychology" challenges universalist developmental accounts, arguing three distinct moral domains—autonomy, community, and divinity—receive different emphasis across cultures. Jonathan Haidt's work formulates findings inviting dialogue with anthropology.

Webb Keane's recent synthesis of psychological, anthropological, and historical perspectives particularly emphasizes emotions and sentiments' foundational role, potentially enriching anthropology of ethics and emotions alike.

## Emerging Debates and Directions

### New Research Areas

Anthropologists now explore how people work to fashion and cultivate ethical qualities through voluntary projects, particularly in religious contexts but extending to parenthood and activism. Renewed interest focuses on concepts of value and values. Following Caroline Humphrey's pioneering work, scholars examine how conduct models based on chosen exemplars—known, historical, or mythical figures—organize ethical life alongside rule-following.

### Global Scope and Special Cases

While occasionally suggested that ethics may not apply in certain regions (Melanesia, Amazonia, extreme poverty), sustained expositions remain absent. Ethnographic accounts of ethical life in such settings exist. China presents densely documented ethical life, with scholars debating whether "moral crisis" concepts illuminate post-Maoist transformations.

### Broader Implications

The ethical turn has renewed anthropological engagement with human agency and social causation, resisting reductive explanations attributing all phenomena to neoliberalism. Analysis of human rights and humanitarianism now incorporates ethical dimensions. Additional topics—happiness, favor-giving, and detachment practices—become analytically rich when approached through ethical frameworks.

### Interdisciplinary Engagement

Anthropology increasingly influences discussions of morality in psychology, moral philosophy, and theology. Symposia on ethical conversations across cultural borders and fundamental sources of ethical life demonstrate productive interdisciplinary potential.

## Conclusion

The anthropology of ethics represents neither a wholly new sub-discipline nor mere rediscovery of old concerns. Rather, it constitutes fundamental rethinking of anthropological theory, revisiting perennial questions through novel frameworks. This development promises enriched understanding of human moral life and productive dialogue with other disciplines engaging ethical questions.
